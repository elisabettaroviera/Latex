\section{Intra-Dataset Exploration and Visualization}
\label{sec:intra_dataset}

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) represents a fundamental step in DNA methylation studies, 
particularly in cancer epigenomics, where alterations in global methylation levels, 
sample-level variability, and locus-specific instability often precede detectable 
phenotypic changes. Before applying any preprocessing, filtering, bias correction, or 
machine-learning models, each dataset must be evaluated independently in order to assess 
its internal structure, detect technical anomalies, and identify biologically meaningful 
patterns.

In the context of breast cancer, epigenetic deregulation manifests through both global and 
local phenomena: genome-wide hypomethylation, focal hypermethylation of tumor-suppressor 
regions, and the presence of pre-neoplastic ``field defects'' in histologically normal 
tissues close to tumors \cite{ehrlich2002, bird2002, teschendorff2016}. 
The primary biological goal of this thesis—\emph{to identify DNA methylation alterations 
in normal tissues and evaluate whether they may contribute to the transition toward 
tumorigenesis}—motivates this detailed intra-dataset investigation.
This section analyzes each dataset (GSE69914 [\ref{subsec:gse69914}], GSE225845 [\ref{subsec:gse225845}], GSE287331 [\ref{subsec:gse287331}]) separately and 
systematically, focusing on:
missingness and quality control, global methylation distributions, sample-level summaries, 
CpG-level instability, correlation structure, dimensionality reduction, and genome-context 
coverage.

% ============================================
% 2.1 GSE69914
% ============================================
\subsection{Dataset GSE69914}
\label{subsec:gse69914}

\paragraph{Dataset overview}
\label{par:gse69914_overview}

The GSE69914 dataset provides genome-wide DNA methylation profiles generated using the 
Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip on bisulfite-converted DNA, covering more than 
480{,}000 CpG sites. The series, contributed by Teschendorff and Widschwendter and publicly 
released on June 18, 2015, is available on GEO under accession 
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE69914}{GSE69914}.

In total, the dataset includes \textbf{407 breast tissue samples} spanning the three tissue states central 
to this thesis:
\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 50 Normal samples, representing the physiological methylation baseline;
    \item 42 Tumor-adjacent samples, reflecting early epigenetic alterations in proximity to the tumor;
    \item 305 Tumor samples, providing the malignant endpoint of the Normal~$\rightarrow$~Adjacent~$\rightarrow$~Tumor axis.
\end{itemize}

A small subset consists of BRCA1-related samples (7 normal carriers and 3 tumors).
BRCA1 encodes a key DNA-repair protein, and pathogenic variants substantially increase lifetime breast and ovarian cancer
risk \cite{brca_factsheet}, adding a hereditary-risk dimension to the cohort.
Since the primary objective of this thesis is to characterise epigenetic differences along the Normal–Adjacent–Tumor
axis that are not driven by strong germline predisposition, BRCA1-mutated samples were excluded from the core exploration
and preprocessing pipeline. Their inclusion would introduce a distinct hereditary subtype with potentially different
baseline methylation patterns, confounding the interpretation of field-effect alterations in sporadic breast cancer.
The final working matrix therefore contains 397 tissue samples and 485{,}512 CpG probes, offering a 
structured and biologically coherent dataset for intra-dataset exploratory analyses.






% ============================================
% Missingness
% ============================================
\paragraph{Missingness and data quality}
\label{par:gse69914_missingness}

An assessment of missing values across all samples and CpG probes confirmed that the 
GSE69914 methylation matrix contains \emph{no missing entries} of any kind. The dataset is 
therefore complete, technically clean, and well suited for direct exploratory analysis and 
visualization without requiring imputation or preliminary data recovery steps.




% ============================================
% Beta distributions
% ============================================

\paragraph{Global methylation distributions}
\label{par:gse69914_global_meth}

To characterise the overall methylation landscape of the dataset, I first examined the 
distribution of $\beta$-values (fractional methylation in $[0,1]$).  
The group-wise mean density curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_group_mean_density}) displays the 
characteristic \emph{bimodal} profile of Illumina 450k arrays, with peaks near unmethylated 
($\beta \approx 0$) and fully methylated ($\beta \approx 1$) CpG sites. This pattern reflects 
the underlying biology of CpG regulation, where many loci tend to be either transcriptionally 
active (hypomethylated) or repressed (hypermethylated) \cite{bird2002, maksimovic2012}.

When comparing tissue groups, a clear gradient emerges.  
Tumor samples show a slightly flatter high-$\beta$ peak and a broader low-$\beta$ tail, 
consistent with the well-described phenomenon of \textbf{global hypomethylation} and increased 
heterogeneity in cancer \cite{ehrlich2002}. Tumor-adjacent samples lie between Normal and 
Tumor, suggesting early epigenetic drift and subtle field effects occurring in histologically 
non-neoplastic tissue \cite{teschendorff2016}. Normal samples exhibit the sharpest and most 
stable bimodality, representing the expected physiological baseline.

% ============================================
% Outlier burden

\paragraph{CpG-level instability and recurrent outliers}
\label{par:gse69914_outliers}

To characterise locus-specific instability, I examined CpG sites that show the strongest 
deviations across samples. The heatmap of the top outlier loci (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_outliers_heatmap}) indicates that epigenetic disruption is 
\emph{not uniform}: specific CpG sites and specific samples display extreme deviations, 
rather than a diffuse genome-wide shift. Moreover, both directions of alteration are 
present — focal \textbf{hypermethylation} (very high $\beta$) and focal 
\textbf{hypomethylation} (very low $\beta$).  
In cancer biology, hypermethylation can silence tumor-suppressor regions, whereas 
hypomethylation can derepress oncogenic pathways and weaken genomic stability, reflecting 
the classic “too much and too little methylation’’ behaviour of tumor genomes 
\cite{ehrlich2002}.

Complementary $\Delta\beta$ distributions comparing Tumor and Adjacent tissues against Normal 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_outliers_deltabeta}) show a clear shift toward 
hypomethylation in Tumor samples and a subtler but detectable drift in Adjacent tissues.  
This provides evidence that epigenetic alterations emerge early in histologically normal 
tissue located near the tumor, supporting the field-cancerization model 
\cite{teschendorff2016}.

\paragraph{Variance and correlation structure}
\label{par:gse69914_variance_corr}
To quantify within-group epigenetic variability, I examined the distribution of CpG-wise
variance across samples. The density curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_var_corr_variance}) show that
Tumor samples have markedly higher variance, reflecting increased epigenetic instability.
In contrast, Normal and Tumor-adjacent samples display almost identical variance
distributions that are tightly concentrated near zero.  
This indicates that, at the level of CpG-wise variability, Adjacent tissues do not yet
exhibit detectable divergence from Normal samples, despite being histologically proximate
to the tumor.

A complementary perspective is provided by the sample correlation heatmap 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_var_corr_correlation}), which shows the expected high level of 
pairwise similarity across all samples. This behaviour is typical of genome-wide DNA 
methylation data, where a large fraction of CpG sites is stable across individuals, 
resulting in consistently strong correlations \cite{teschendorff2013}.

\paragraph{Low-dimensional embeddings}
\label{par:gse69914_embeddings}

Dimensionality reduction was applied using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP to visualise global 
similarities among samples (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_embeddings_combined}).  
Across all three methods, Normal and Tumor-adjacent samples show substantial overlap, 
with no clear separation between these two groups. Tumor samples appear more dispersed, 
but only mildly shifted relative to the Normal/Adjacent cluster, and no sharp cluster 
boundaries emerge in any embedding.

Overall, these projections indicate that global methylation patterns alone do not 
provide strong discriminative structure among the three tissue states. This suggests 
that group differences are subtle at the whole-methylome level and are better captured 
through locus-specific analyses (e.g., outlier burden or $\Delta\beta$), rather than 
through global unsupervised embeddings.

% ============================================
% Manifest coverage
% ============================================
\paragraph{Genome annotation coverage}
\label{par:gse69914_manifest}

CpG probes were annotated using the official \textit{Infinium MethylationEPIC v1.0 B5} \cite{illumina_manifest_epic}
manifest file, the standard Illumina resource containing genomic coordinates, probe type, 
CpG island context (Island, Shore, Shelf, Open Sea), and gene-level annotations.  
Using this manifest, the GSE69914 dataset shows the expected non-uniform genomic 
distribution of CpG contexts: the largest fraction of probes maps to open-sea or 
non-island regions, followed by CpG islands, then north and south shores, and finally 
north and south shelves. This ordering mirrors the characteristic architecture of the 
EPIC/HM450 arrays reported in the literature.  
Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2017} describe the EPIC design and its inheritance of the HM450 
probe structure, while Pidsley et al.~\cite{pidsley2016} document the enrichment of 
probes in CpG islands and shores with decreasing density in shelves and open-sea regions.  
The coverage observed in GSE69914 is therefore consistent with the known genomic 
composition of the Illumina methylation arrays, supporting correct manifest alignment.


% ============================================
% Dynamic network proxies
% ============================================
\paragraph{Dynamic-network proxies}
\label{par:gse69914_dnb_proxies}

Although this analysis does not follow the full mathematical formalism of Dynamic Network 
Biomarkers (DNBs), the degree-based and network-level representations in 
Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_combined} provide a qualitative view of how methylation 
perturbations may organize at the network level.  
DNB theory, originally formulated for gene-expression and regulatory networks, predicts 
that near a critical transition a subset of components displays increased internal 
fluctuations and coordinated behaviour \cite{chen2012dnb, west2018entropy, liu2022lne}.  
Here I adapt this conceptual framework to CpG-level methylation patterns by examining the 
structure of CpG–gene degree distributions and the organization of $\Delta\beta$-driven 
network layouts. This allows me to explore whether tumour-associated instability manifests 
as locally coordinated methylation shifts at the network level, even without implementing 
the complete DNB pipeline.

The degree–rank plot (Fig. \ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_degree}) displays a heavy-tailed distribution of CpG counts per
gene, consistent with the heterogeneous hub–periphery structure typically observed in
biological systems.  
In the Tumor~vs~Normal comparison (Fig. \ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_tumor_normal}), a compact nucleus of nodes with
moderately high degree emerges, accompanied by larger $\Delta\beta$ deviations.  
This pattern suggests a more perturbed and locally coordinated subset of CpG/gene units in
tumor tissue, qualitatively consistent with increased network instability.  
In contrast, the Adjacent~vs~Normal network (Fig. \ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_adjacent_normal}) lacks a comparable hub-like
structure and shows smaller, more diffuse deviations, indicating that the adjacent tissue
may exhibit only early or weakly organized perturbations.

Although these observations do not satisfy the full mathematical criteria for identifying
a DNB module, they align conceptually with the idea that tumor tissue occupies a more
unstable network regime, whereas adjacent tissue remains closer to the stable
(pre-transition) configuration.

% ============================================
% Summary
% ============================================
\paragraph{Overall interpretation}
The exploratory analysis of GSE69914 reveals a consistent but overall subtle separation 
between the three tissue states. Normal and Tumor-adjacent samples share highly similar 
global methylation profiles, variance distributions, correlation structure, and 
low-dimensional embeddings, indicating that large-scale methylome organisation remains 
largely conserved in adjacent tissue. Tumor samples show the expected increase in 
heterogeneity and a shift toward hypomethylation, but these differences remain modest at 
the whole-methylome level and do not generate clear separation in unsupervised embeddings.

Locus-specific analyses, however, highlight focused patterns of disruption. Outlier CpGs 
show both hyper- and hypomethylation events, and $\Delta\beta$ distributions reveal a 
distinct hypomethylation bias in Tumor samples and a milder drift in Adjacent tissue. 
Dynamic-network proxies further suggest a more compact and perturbed core in Tumor 
samples, whereas Adjacent tissue remains more diffuse and weakly perturbed.

Altogether, GSE69914 presents a technically clean dataset with well-structured methylation 
patterns and biologically interpretable deviations. The results indicate that tumour-related 
alterations emerge predominantly at focal CpG loci rather than at the global methylome level, 
and that Adjacent tissue reflects early but subtle perturbations consistent with 
field-effect phenomena.

\subsection{Dataset GSE225845}
\label{subsec:gse225845}

\paragraph{Dataset overview}
\label{par:gse225845_overview}

The GSE225845 dataset provides genome-wide DNA methylation profiles generated using the
Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (850K) on bisulfite-converted DNA, covering more
than 850{,}000 CpG sites. The series, part of the NCI-Maryland Breast Cancer Cohort and
publicly released on October 15, 2023, is available on GEO under accession
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE225845}{GSE225845}.

In its original design, the study profiled \textbf{402 fresh-frozen breast tissues from 289 women},
including 185 tumor samples, 113 paired adjacent normal samples, and 104 normal tissues from
reduction mammoplasty. Neighbourhood-level socioeconomic deprivation was quantified via a
Neighborhood Deprivation Index (NDI) and linked to DNA methylation and gene-expression
changes in cancer- and immune-related pathways.

For the purposes of this thesis, I focus on the three tissue states central to the
Normal~$\rightarrow$~Adjacent~$\rightarrow$~Tumor axis using the harmonized working matrix.
After quality control and label harmonization, this matrix includes \textbf{477 breast tissue samples}:
\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 113 Normal samples, representing the physiological methylation baseline;
    \item 140 Tumor-adjacent samples, reflecting early epigenetic alterations in proximity to the tumor;
    \item 224 Tumor samples, providing the malignant endpoint of the Normal~$\rightarrow$~Adjacent~$\rightarrow$~Tumor axis.
\end{itemize}

The dataset also includes detailed metadata on demographic and clinical covariates
(e.g., age at surgery, race, sex, tissue descriptors).

The final working methylation matrix used in the exploratory analyses therefore contains
\textbf{477 tissue samples and 750{,}426 CpG probes}, providing a high-resolution EPIC-based dataset for
intra-dataset exploratory analyses.





% ============================================
% Missingness
% ============================================
\paragraph{Missingness and data quality}
\label{par:gse225845_missingness}

Missingness assessment shows a fully complete matrix: both samples and CpG probes have
zero median missingness, and no entries exceed standard QC thresholds. Accordingly, the
dataset requires \emph{no imputation or corrective preprocessing} at this stage and is
technically well suited for direct exploratory analysis.


% ============================================

\paragraph{Global methylation distributions}
\label{par:gse225845_global_meth}

The group-wise mean $\beta$-value densities (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse225845_group_mean_density})
exhibit the characteristic bimodal profile of EPIC arrays, with peaks near unmethylated
($\beta \approx 0$) and fully methylated ($\beta \approx 1$) CpG sites. Across the three
tissue groups, the curves are nearly superimposed, indicating highly similar global
methylation landscapes.

Normal and Adjacent samples show essentially identical profiles, while Tumor samples
display only minimal deviations—slightly higher density at low $\beta$ values and a very
subtle decrease in the high-$\beta$ peak. Overall, the dataset appears globally homogeneous,
with group differences likely emerging at more localized, CpG-specific scales rather than
at the whole-methylome level.

\paragraph{CpG-level instability and recurrent outliers}
\label{par:gse225845_outliers}

To characterise locus-specific instability, I examined the 200 CpG sites with the highest
outlier burden across samples. The heatmap of these loci
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse225845_outliers_heatmap}) shows that instability in this subset is
highly \emph{directional}: almost all recurrent outlier events correspond to very low
$\beta$ values, indicating focal \textbf{hypomethylation}. In other words, within the
top outlier CpGs, the dominant perturbation pattern is a consistent loss of methylation
across many samples. This reflects instability concentrated on a small set of CpGs rather
than a diffuse genome-wide effect.

The $\Delta\beta$ distributions comparing Tumor and Adjacent tissues against Normal
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse225845_outliers_deltabeta}) are sharply peaked around zero but display
a clearly heavier tail for $\Delta\beta < 0$ than for $\Delta\beta > 0$. Tumor samples
show the longest negative tail, consistent with more pronounced hypomethylation, while
Adjacent tissues exhibit a milder yet still detectable excess of hypomethylated loci.
This pattern emphasizes the “too little methylation’’ component of cancer-associated
epigenetic change \cite{ehrlich2002} and suggests that adjacent tissue already shares,
in attenuated form, the same hypomethylation bias observed in tumors, in line with a
field-effect model \cite{teschendorff2016}.

(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse225845_var_corr_variance}) show that the three groups exhibit distinct
levels of variability. Normal samples display the lowest and most concentrated variances,
whereas Tumor samples present a clearly longer tail, indicating a subset of CpGs with
higher instability. Adjacent samples differ from Normal but do not follow a simple

The sample–sample correlation heatmap

positive across the dataset. Most values fall within a narrow positive range, indicating a
globally homogeneous methylation background with only mild fluctuations between samples.
No strong blocks of high or low correlation are visible, suggesting that differences arise
from localized CpG-specific variation rather than large-scale shifts in the global
methylation structure.

\paragraph{Low-dimensional embeddings}
\label{par:gse225845_embeddings}

Dimensionality reduction was applied using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP to visualise the global
organisation of the samples.  
The PCA (Fig.~\ref{fig:pca_gse225845}) plot shows a strong overall overlap among the three groups, with only a mild
elongation of the Tumor samples along the second principal component. No clear separation
emerges at this linear scale.

Nonlinear embeddings reveal more structure. In t-SNE space (Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne_gse225845}), Normal and Adjacent samples
form partially separated regions, although both groups contain scattered outliers. Tumor
samples are markedly more dispersed and extend over a wider area, reflecting higher global
heterogeneity; several Tumor samples appear as isolated points far from the main cloud.

The UMAP (Fig.~\ref{fig:umap_gse225845}) embedding displays a similar pattern: Normal and Adjacent samples are relatively

\paragraph{Overall interpretation}

The exploratory analysis of GSE225845 reveals that, at a global scale, the three tissue
states share highly similar methylation profiles. Mean $\beta$-value densities are almost
superimposed across groups, and both sample–sample correlations and PCA projections show a
substantial degree of overlap. These results indicate that large-scale methylome structure
is broadly conserved across Normal, Adjacent, and Tumor tissues, with group differences not
manifesting as broad genome-wide shifts.

Local analyses, however, reveal clearer distinctions. Among the 200 CpGs with the highest
outlier burden, instability is strongly directional: the dominant pattern is focal
hypomethylation, especially in Tumor samples, with Adjacent tissues showing a milder but
consistent excess of negative $\Delta\beta$ values. Variance distributions further support
this picture, with Tumor samples exhibiting a heavier tail and Adjacent tissues differing
from Normal without forming a simple intermediate pattern.

Nonlinear embeddings (t-SNE and UMAP) highlight differences in dispersion rather than
cluster separation: Normal and Adjacent samples form relatively compact regions with some
outliers, whereas Tumor samples are more widely spread, reflecting increased heterogeneity.
Finally, network-based proxies show that Tumor–Normal contrasts produce a compact central
nucleus of perturbed CpG–gene units, while Adjacent–Normal differences appear more
fragmented and diffuse.

Overall, GSE225845 emerges as a technically clean and globally homogeneous dataset in which
tissue-state differences are subtle at the whole-methylome level but become more apparent
when focusing on localized CpG-specific perturbations, dispersion patterns, and small-scale
network structure.

\subsection{Dataset GSE287331}

\paragraph{Dataset overview}
\label{par:gse287331_overview}

The GSE287331 dataset provides genome-wide DNA methylation profiles generated using the 
Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v1.0 BeadChip on bisulfite-converted breast tissue DNA, 
covering more than 700{,}000 CpG sites (702{,}573 CpGs in the working matrix). 
The series, contributed by Dennis \textit{et al.} and publicly released on June 18, 2025, is 
available on GEO under accession 
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE287331}{GSE287331}.  

The original study is organised along a ``tumor proximity axis'' (TPxA), spanning healthy 
donated breast tissue from cancer-unaffected donors (HDB), contralateral unaffected breast 
(CUB), ipsilateral opposite quadrant (OQ), tumor-adjacent normal tissue (AN), and tumor (TU) 
samples. 

Overall, the dataset comprises \textbf{446 breast tissue samples} distributed across the 
five tissue categories listed below. The initial phase of the analysis investigates all 
five groups separately. Subsequently, in order to ensure comparability with the other 
GEO series, the working cohort is restricted to the three main labels of interest 
(Normal, Adjacent, Tumor) by retaining only HDB, AN and TU samples and discarding 
CUB and OQ.

\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 182 HDB samples (healthy donated breast), representing cancer-unaffected breast tissue from donors and used as the physiological methylation baseline;
    \item 60 AN samples (adjacent-normal), corresponding to histologically normal tissue sampled near the tumor and capturing early epigenetic alterations along the tumor-proximity axis;
    \item 69 TU samples (tumor tissue), representing invasive breast cancer and defining the malignant endpoint of the axis;
    \item 67 OQ samples (ipsilateral opposite quadrant), benign tissue from the same breast but distant from the tumor, reflecting intermediate proximity-associated alterations;
    \item 68 CUB samples (contralateral unaffected breast), benign tissue from the opposite breast, representing a distal but still patient-matched non-malignant reference.
\end{itemize}


All methylation levels are stored as $\beta$-values in $[0,1]$, 
yielding a compact working matrix of 446 tissue samples by 702{,}573 CpG probes that is 
well suited for large-scale exploratory analysis and downstream modelling.

\paragraph{Missingness filtering and imputation}
\label{par:gse287331_missingness}

Before conducting any exploratory analysis, I quantified the amount and structure of
missing values in the raw GSE287331 $\beta$-matrix. Probe-wise missingness is a well-known
source of unreliability in Illumina methylation arrays, and several studies recommend
removing CpGs that exhibit low measurement quality or an excessive proportion of missing
values. For example, Islam \textit{et al.}~\cite{islam2019_pediatric_dnamm} excluded probes
with missing $\beta$-values in more than $2\%$ of samples in their pediatric tissue
methylation analysis, while Mansell \textit{et al.}~\cite{mansell2019guidance} applied a
similar $1\%$ threshold for detection failure in their EPIC-array quality control
pipeline. 

Guided by this evidence and the large number of CpG sites available in GSE287331
($>700{,}000$ probes), I adopted a stringent probe-wise filtering criterion and removed all
CpGs missing in more than $1\%$ of samples. This choice prioritises high-confidence
measurements while minimising reliance on imputed values.

After filtering, only a negligible fraction of values remained missing in the working
matrix (58\,561 NaN, corresponding to $0.0187\%$ of all entries). Given this extremely low
level of sparsity, median-based imputation is both statistically robust and
distribution-preserving. Following current recommendations, where missing methylation values
are replaced by probe-wise medians to avoid introducing group-specific bias, all residual
NaN were imputed using the median $\beta$-value of each CpG across samples. This approach is consistent with recent harmonization methods such as mLiftOver, 
where missing methylation values are explicitly replaced using probe-wise median 
substitution to preserve biologically plausible levels \cite{chen2024_mliftover}.

\paragraph{Global methylation distributions}

distribution of $\beta$-values (fractional methylation in $[0,1]$).

\paragraph{Dynamic-network proxies}

Although this analysis does not follow the full mathematical formalism of Dynamic Network 
Biomarkers (DNBs), the degree-based and network-level representations in 
Fig.~\ref{fig:gse287331_dnb_combined} provide a qualitative view of how methylation 
perturbations may organize at the network level.  
DNB theory, originally formulated for gene-expression and regulatory networks, predicts 
that near a critical transition a subset of components displays increased internal 
fluctuations and coordinated behaviour \cite{chen2012dnb, west2018entropy, liu2022lne}.  
Here I adapt this conceptual framework to CpG-level methylation patterns by examining the 
structure of CpG–gene degree distributions and the organization of $\Delta\beta$-driven 
network layouts. This allows me to explore whether tumour-associated instability manifests 
as locally coordinated methylation shifts at the network level, even without implementing 
the complete DNB pipeline.

The degree–rank plot (Fig. \ref{fig:gse287331_dnb_degree}) displays a heavy-tailed distribution of CpG counts per
gene, consistent with the heterogeneous hub–periphery structure typically observed in
biological systems.  
In the Tumor~vs~Normal comparison (Fig. \ref{fig:gse287331_dnb_tumor_normal}), a compact nucleus of nodes with
moderately high degree emerges, accompanied by larger $\Delta\beta$ deviations.  
This pattern suggests a more perturbed and locally coordinated subset of CpG/gene units in
tumor tissue, qualitatively consistent with increased network instability.  
In contrast, the Adjacent~vs~Normal network (Fig. \ref{fig:gse287331_dnb_adjacent_normal}) lacks a comparable hub-like
structure and shows smaller, more diffuse deviations, indicating that the adjacent tissue
may exhibit only early or weakly organized perturbations.

Although these observations do not satisfy the full mathematical criteria for identifying
a DNB module, they align conceptually with the idea that tumor tissue occupies a more
unstable network regime, whereas adjacent tissue remains closer to the stable
(pre-transition) configuration.

\paragraph{Summary}
The exploratory analysis of GSE287331 reveals a consistent but overall subtle separation 
between the three tissue states. Normal and Tumor-adjacent samples share highly similar 
global methylation profiles, variance distributions, correlation structure, and 
low-dimensional embeddings, indicating that large-scale methylome organisation remains 
largely conserved in adjacent tissue. Tumor samples show the expected increase in 
heterogeneity and a shift toward hypomethylation, but these differences remain modest at 
the whole-methylome level and do not generate clear separation in unsupervised embeddings.

Locus-specific analyses, however, highlight focused patterns of disruption. Outlier CpGs 
show both hyper- and hypomethylation events, and $\Delta\beta$ distributions reveal a 
distinct hypomethylation bias in Tumor samples and a milder drift in Adjacent tissue. 
Dynamic-network proxies further suggest a more compact and perturbed core in Tumor 
samples, whereas Adjacent tissue remains more diffuse and weakly perturbed.

Altogether, GSE287331 presents a technically clean dataset with well-structured methylation 
patterns and biologically interpretable deviations. The results indicate that tumour-related 
alterations emerge predominantly at focal CpG loci rather than at the global methylome level, 
and that Adjacent tissue reflects early but subtle perturbations consistent with 
field-effect phenomena.
