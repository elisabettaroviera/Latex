\begin{abstract}
This document collects the exploratory analyses carried out on the three breast–tissue DNA methylation
datasets employed in this thesis (GSE69914, GSE225845, GSE287331).  
The aim is to characterise the Normal $\rightarrow$ Adjacent $\rightarrow$ Tumor methylation axis within
each cohort and to evaluate the reproducibility of early epigenetic drift across independent studies.

All analyses were performed through a set of fully reproducible Jupyter notebooks (R or Python), available in the 
\href{https://github.com/elisabettaroviera/THESIS/tree/main}{\texttt{thesis GitHub repository}}:

\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 
    \href{https://github.com/elisabettaroviera/THESIS/blob/main/01%20-%20Notebook/01%20-%20Data%20Exploration%20%26%20Visualization/a%20-%20Intra%20Dataset/01-data-exploration-visualization-gse69914.ipynb}{\textbf{01-data-exploration-visualization-GSE69914.ipynb}}  
    -- Examines global methylation distributions, CpG-level instability, outlier burden, variance structure, 
    and embedding-based group separation for the HM450 GSE69914 cohort.

    \item 
    \href{https://github.com/elisabettaroviera/THESIS/blob/main/01%20-%20Notebook/01%20-%20Data%20Exploration%20%26%20Visualization/a%20-%20Intra%20Dataset/01-data-exploration-visualization-gse225845.ipynb}{\textbf{01-data-exploration-visualization-GSE225845.ipynb}  }  
    -- Provides an analogous exploratory analysis for the EPIC-based GSE225845 dataset, including 
    $\Delta\beta$ distributions, outlier profiles, multivariate embeddings, and network-level proxies of instability.

    \item  
    \href{https://github.com/elisabettaroviera/THESIS/blob/main/01%20-%20Notebook/01%20-%20Data%20Exploration%20%26%20Visualization/a%20-%20Intra%20Dataset/01-data-exploration-visualization-gse287331.ipynb}{\textbf{01-data-exploration-visualization-GSE287331.ipynb} }  
    -- Performs the same analyses on the high-resolution EPIC dataset GSE287331, revealing the strongest 
    Normal–Adjacent–Tumor separation among the three cohorts.

    \item 
    \href{https://github.com/elisabettaroviera/THESIS/blob/main/01%20-%20Notebook/01%20-%20Data%20Exploration%20%26%20Visualization/b%20-%20Inter%20Dataset/01-data-explorationvisualization-inter-analysis.ipynb}{\textbf{01-data-explorationvisualization-inter-analysis.ipynb} }  
    -- Compares Normal and Adjacent tissues across all three datasets, quantifying shared-CpG structure, 
    $\Delta\beta$ concordance, variance patterns, and PCA/t-SNE embedding alignment.

    \item   
    \href{https://github.com/elisabettaroviera/THESIS/blob/main/01%20-%20Notebook/01%20-%20Data%20Exploration%20%26%20Visualization/01-correction-bias-type-i-vs-type-ii.ipynb}{\textbf{01-correction-bias-type-i-vs-type-ii.ipynb}}  
    -- Implements probe-design bias diagnostics and BMIQ normalization for GSE287331, reconstructing a 
    Type~I/II–balanced $\beta$-matrix suitable for inter-cohort comparability and downstream modelling.

\end{itemize}

Collectively, these notebooks provide a unified, reproducible exploration framework that highlights both
shared and dataset-specific features of the Normal–Adjacent–Tumor axis.  
They form the methodological foundation for the subsequent discovery of early epigenetic alterations in
Adjacent tissue and for their replication across independent cohorts.
\end{abstract}



\section{Intra-Dataset Exploration and Visualization}
\label{sec:intra_dataset}

Exploratory Data Analysis represents a fundamental step in DNA methylation studies, 
particularly in cancer epigenomics, where alterations in global methylation levels, 
sample-level variability, and locus-specific instability often precede detectable 
phenotypic changes. Before applying any preprocessing, filtering, or 
machine-learning models, each dataset must be evaluated independently in order to assess 
its internal structure, detect technical anomalies, and identify biologically meaningful 
patterns.

In the context of breast cancer, epigenetic deregulation manifests through both global and 
local phenomena: genome-wide hypomethylation, focal hypermethylation of tumor-suppressor 
regions, and the presence of pre-neoplastic ``field defects'' in histologically normal 
tissues close to tumors \cite{ehrlich2002, bird2002, teschendorff2016}. 
The primary biological goal of this thesis—\emph{to identify DNA methylation alterations 
in normal tissues and evaluate whether they may contribute to the transition toward 
tumorigenesis}—motivates this detailed intra-dataset investigation.
This section analyzes each dataset (GSE69914 [\ref{subsec:gse69914}], GSE225845 [\ref{subsec:gse225845}], GSE287331 [\ref{subsec:gse287331}]) separately and 
systematically, focusing on:
missingness and quality control, global methylation distributions, sample-level summaries, 
CpG-level instability, correlation structure, dimensionality reduction, and genome-context 
coverage.

% ============================================
% 2.1 GSE69914
% ============================================
\subsection{Dataset GSE69914}
\label{subsec:gse69914}

\paragraph{Dataset overview}
\label{par:gse69914_overview}

The GSE69914 dataset provides genome-wide DNA methylation profiles generated using the 
Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip on bisulfite-converted DNA, covering more than 
480{,}000 CpG sites. The series, contributed by Teschendorff and Widschwendter and publicly 
released on June 18, 2015, is available on GEO under accession 
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE69914}{GSE69914}.

In total, the dataset includes \textbf{407 breast tissue samples} spanning the three tissue states central 
to this thesis:
\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 50 Normal samples, representing the physiological methylation baseline;
    \item 42 Adjacent samples, reflecting early epigenetic alterations in proximity to the tumor;
    \item 305 Tumor samples, providing the malignant endpoint of the Normal~$\rightarrow$~Adjacent~$\rightarrow$~Tumor axis.
\end{itemize}

A small subset consists of BRCA1-related samples (7 normal carriers and 3 tumors).
BRCA1 encodes a key DNA-repair protein, and pathogenic variants substantially increase lifetime breast and ovarian cancer
risk \cite{brca_factsheet}, adding a hereditary-risk dimension to the cohort.
Since the primary objective of this thesis is to characterise epigenetic differences along the Normal–Adjacent
axis that are not driven by strong germline predisposition, BRCA1-mutated samples were excluded from the core exploration
and preprocessing pipeline, following established evidence that BRCA1-associated tissues constitute a distinct hereditary epigenetic profile \cite{Bartlett2022,Suijkerbuijk2008}.
Their inclusion would introduce samples with potentially different baseline methylation patterns, confounding the interpretation of field-effect alterations in sporadic breast cancer.
The final working matrix therefore contains 397 tissue samples and 485{,}512 CpG probes.

\paragraph{Missingness and data quality}
\label{par:gse69914_missingness}

The GSE69914 methylation matrix contains \emph{no missing entries} of any kind. 



% ============================================
% Beta distributions
% ============================================

\paragraph{Global methylation distributions}
\label{par:gse69914_global_meth}

To characterise the overall methylation landscape of the dataset, I first examined the 
distribution of $\beta$-values (fractional methylation in $[0,1]$).  
The group-wise mean density curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_group_mean_density}) displays the 
characteristic \emph{bimodal} profile of Illumina 450k arrays, with peaks near unmethylated 
($\beta \approx 0$) and fully methylated ($\beta \approx 1$) CpG sites. This pattern reflects 
the underlying biology of CpG regulation, where many loci tend to be either transcriptionally 
active (hypomethylated) or repressed (hypermethylated) \cite{bird2002, maksimovic2012}.

When comparing tissue groups, a clear gradient emerges.  
Tumor samples show a slightly flatter high-$\beta$ peak and a broader low-$\beta$ tail, 
consistent with the well-described phenomenon of \textbf{global hypomethylation} and increased 
heterogeneity in cancer \cite{ehrlich2002}. Adjacent samples lie between Normal and 
Tumor, suggesting early epigenetic drift and subtle field effects occurring in histologically 
non-neoplastic tissue \cite{teschendorff2016}.

\paragraph{CpG-level instability and recurrent outliers}
\label{par:gse69914_outliers}

To characterise locus-specific instability, I examined the 200 CpG sites with the highest
outlier burden across samples. The heatmap of the top outlier loci (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_outliers_heatmap}) indicates that epigenetic disruption is 
\emph{not uniform}: specific CpG sites and specific samples display extreme deviations, 
rather than a diffuse genome-wide shift. Moreover, both directions of alteration are 
present — focal \textbf{hypermethylation} and focal 
\textbf{hypomethylation}.  
In cancer biology, hypermethylation can silence tumor-suppressor regions, whereas 
hypomethylation can derepress oncogenic pathways and weaken genomic stability, reflecting 
the classic “too much and too little methylation’’ behaviour of tumor genomes 
\cite{ehrlich2002}.

Complementary $\Delta\beta$ distributions comparing Tumor and Adjacent tissues against Normal 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_outliers_deltabeta}) show a clear shift toward 
hypomethylation in Tumor samples and a subtler but detectable drift in Adjacent tissues.  
This provides evidence that epigenetic alterations emerge early in histologically normal 
tissue located near the tumor, supporting the field-cancerization model 
\cite{teschendorff2016}.

\paragraph{Variance and correlation structure}
\label{par:gse69914_variance_corr}
To quantify within-group epigenetic variability, I examined the distribution of CpG-wise
variance across samples. The density curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_var_corr_variance}) show that
Tumor samples have markedly higher variance, reflecting increased epigenetic instability.
In contrast, Normal and Adjacent samples display almost identical variance
distributions that are tightly concentrated near zero.  
This indicates that, at the level of CpG-wise variability, Adjacent tissues do not yet
exhibit detectable divergence from Normal samples.

A complementary perspective is provided by the sample correlation heatmap 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_var_corr_correlation}), which shows the expected high level of 
pairwise similarity across all samples. This behaviour is typical of genome-wide DNA 
methylation data, where a large fraction of CpG sites is stable across individuals, 
resulting in consistently strong correlations \cite{Teschendorff2013BMIQ}. 
Importantly, the heatmap also reveals that all samples are highly mutually correlated,
with no sharp blocks or boundaries separating the three tissue labels. This confirms that 
global methylation structure is largely conserved across Normal, Adjacent and Tumor tissues,
and that group differences are too subtle to be detected through 
sample–sample correlation alone.

\paragraph{Low-dimensional embeddings}
\label{par:gse69914_embeddings}

Dimensionality reduction was applied using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP to visualise global 
similarities among samples (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_embeddings_combined}).  
Across all three methods, Normal and Adjacent samples show substantial overlap, 
with no clear separation between these two groups. Tumor samples appear more dispersed, 
but only mildly shifted relative to the Normal/Adjacent cluster, and no sharp cluster 
boundaries emerge in any embedding.

These projections indicate that global methylation patterns alone do not 
provide strong discriminative structure among the three tissue states. This suggests 
that group differences are subtle at the whole-methylome level and are better captured 
through locus-specific analyses, rather than 
through global unsupervised embeddings.

% Manifest coverage

\paragraph{Genome annotation coverage}
\label{par:gse69914_manifest}

CpG probes were annotated using the official \textit{Infinium MethylationEPIC v1.0 B5} \cite{illumina_manifest_epic}
manifest file, the standard Illumina resource containing genomic coordinates, probe type, 
CpG island context (Island, Shore, Shelf, Open Sea), and gene-level annotations.  
Using this manifest, the dataset shows the expected non-uniform genomic 
distribution of CpG contexts: the largest fraction of probes maps to open-sea or 
non-island regions, followed by CpG islands, then north and south shores, and finally 
north and south shelves. This ordering mirrors the characteristic architecture of the 
HM450 arrays reported in the literature~\cite{zhou2017, pidsley2016}.  
The coverage observed is therefore consistent with the known genomic 
composition of the Illumina methylation arrays, supporting correct manifest alignment.

\paragraph{Dynamic-network proxies}
\label{par:gse69914_dnb_proxies}

Although this analysis does not follow the full mathematical formalism of Dynamic Network 
Biomarkers (DNBs), the degree-based and network-level representations in 
Fig.~\ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_combined} provide a qualitative view of how methylation 
perturbations may organize at the network level.  
DNB theory, originally formulated for gene-expression and regulatory networks, predicts 
that near a critical transition a subset of components displays increased internal 
fluctuations and coordinated behaviour \cite{chen2012dnb, west2018entropy, liu2022lne}.  
Here I adapt this conceptual framework to CpG-level methylation patterns by examining the 
structure of CpG–gene degree distributions and the organization of $\Delta\beta$-driven 
network layouts. This allows me to explore whether tumour-associated instability manifests 
as locally coordinated methylation shifts at the network level, even without implementing 
the complete DNB pipeline.

The degree–rank plot (Fig. \ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_degree}) displays a heavy-tailed distribution of CpG counts per
gene, consistent with the heterogeneous hub–periphery structure typically observed in
biological systems.  
In the Tumor~vs~Normal comparison (Fig. \ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_tumor_normal}), a compact nucleus of nodes with
moderately high degree emerges, accompanied by larger $\Delta\beta$ deviations.  
This pattern suggests a more perturbed and locally coordinated subset of CpG/gene units in
tumor tissue, qualitatively consistent with increased network instability.  
In contrast, the Adjacent~vs~Normal network (Fig. \ref{fig:gse69914_dnb_adjacent_normal}) lacks a comparable hub-like
structure and shows smaller, more diffuse deviations, indicating that the adjacent tissue
may exhibit only early or weakly organized perturbations.

Although these observations do not satisfy the full mathematical criteria for identifying
a DNB module, they align conceptually with the idea that tumor tissue occupies a more
unstable network regime, whereas adjacent tissue remains closer to the stable
(pre-transition) configuration.

% ============================================
% Summary
% ============================================
\paragraph{Overall interpretation}
The exploratory analysis of GSE69914 reveals a consistent but overall subtle separation 
between the three tissue states. Normal and Adjacent samples share highly similar 
global methylation profiles, variance distributions, correlation structure, and 
low-dimensional embeddings, indicating that large-scale methylome organisation remains 
largely conserved in Adjacent tissue. Across these global summaries, the two groups are 
so intermixed that no clear or marked distinction emerges between Normal and Adjacent 
samples when using these whole-methylome descriptors. Tumor samples show the expected 
increase in heterogeneity and a shift toward hypomethylation, but these differences remain 
modest at the whole-methylome level and do not generate clear separation in unsupervised 
embeddings.

Locus-specific analyses, however, highlight focused patterns of disruption. Outlier CpGs 
show both hyper- and hypomethylation events, and $\Delta\beta$ distributions reveal a 
distinct hypomethylation bias in Tumor samples and a milder drift in Adjacent tissue. 
Dynamic-network proxies further suggest a more compact and perturbed core in Tumor 
samples, whereas Adjacent tissue remains more diffuse and weakly perturbed.

Altogether, GSE69914 presents a technically clean dataset with well-structured methylation 
patterns and biologically interpretable deviations. The results indicate that tumour-related 
alterations emerge predominantly at focal CpG loci rather than at the global methylome level, 
and that Adjacent tissue reflects early but subtle perturbations consistent with 
field-effect phenomena.

\subsection{Dataset GSE225845}
\label{subsec:gse225845}

\paragraph{Dataset overview}
\label{par:gse225845_overview}

The GSE225845 dataset provides genome-wide DNA methylation profiles generated using the
Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (850K) on bisulfite-converted DNA, covering more
than 850{,}000 CpG sites. The series, part of the NCI-Maryland Breast Cancer Cohort and
publicly released on October 15, 2023, is available on GEO under accession
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE225845}{GSE225845}.

In its original design, the study profiled \textbf{402 fresh-frozen breast tissues from 289 women},
including 185 Tumor samples, 113 paired Adjacent normal samples, and 104 Normal tissues from
reduction mammoplasty. Neighbourhood-level socioeconomic deprivation was quantified via a
Neighborhood Deprivation Index (NDI) and linked to DNA methylation and gene-expression
changes in cancer- and immune-related pathways.

For the purposes of this thesis, I focus on the three tissue states central to the
Normal~$\rightarrow$~Adjacent~$\rightarrow$~Tumor axis using the harmonized working matrix.
After quality control and label harmonization, this matrix includes \textbf{477 breast tissue samples}:
\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 113 Normal samples, representing the physiological methylation baseline;
    \item 140 Adjacent samples, reflecting early epigenetic alterations in proximity to the tumor;
    \item 224 Tumor samples, providing the malignant endpoint of the Normal~$\rightarrow$~Adjacent~$\rightarrow$~Tumor axis.
\end{itemize}

The dataset also includes detailed metadata on demographic and clinical covariates
(e.g., age at surgery, race, sex, tissue descriptors). The final working methylation matrix used in the exploratory analyses therefore contains
\textbf{477 tissue samples and 750{,}426 CpG probes}.

tissue groups, the curves are nearly superimposed, indicating highly similar global
methylation landscapes.

Normal and Adjacent samples show essentially identical profiles, while Tumor samples
display only minimal deviations—slightly higher density at low $\beta$ values and a very
subtle decrease in the high-$\beta$ peak.

\paragraph{Overall interpretation}

The exploratory analysis of GSE225845 reveals that, at a global scale, the three tissue states
share highly similar methylation profiles. Mean $\beta$-value densities are almost superimposed
across groups, and both sample--sample correlations and PCA projections show a substantial degree
of overlap. These results indicate that large-scale methylome structure is broadly conserved across
Normal, Adjacent, and Tumor tissues, with group differences not manifesting as broad genome-wide
shifts.

Local analyses, however, reveal clearer distinctions. Among the 200 CpGs with the highest outlier
burden, instability is strongly directional: the dominant pattern is focal hypomethylation. 

Nonlinear embeddings (t-SNE and UMAP) highlight differences in dispersion rather than discrete
cluster separation: Normal and Adjacent samples form relatively compact regions with only limited
displacement, whereas Tumor samples are more widely spread, reflecting increased heterogeneity.
Network-based proxies similarly show that Tumor--Normal contrasts produce a compact central nucleus
of perturbed CpG--gene units, while Adjacent--Normal differences appear more fragmented and diffuse.

Despite being the dataset with the largest number of samples among the three cohorts---providing
substantial statistical power and highly stable estimates of global methylation structure---the
separation between Normal and Adjacent tissues remains only modestly apparent. The Normal--Adjacent
contrast is therefore not strongly marked, reinforcing the interpretation that early
epigenetic drift in this cohort is subtle and primarily focal rather than globally disruptive.

Overall, GSE225845 emerges as a technically clean and globally homogeneous dataset in which
tissue-state differences are subtle at the whole-methylome level but become more apparent when
focusing on localized CpG-specific perturbations, dispersion patterns, and small-scale network
structure.

\subsection{Dataset GSE287331}
\label{subsec:gse287331}

\paragraph{Dataset overview}
\label{par:gse287331_overview}

The GSE287331 dataset provides genome-wide DNA methylation profiles generated using the 
Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v1.0 BeadChip on bisulfite-converted breast tissue DNA. 
The series, contributed by Dennis \textit{et al.} \cite{Lord2024GSE287331} and publicly released on June 18, 2025, is 
available on GEO under accession 
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE287331}{GSE287331}.  

The original study is organised along a ``tumor proximity axis'' (TPxA), spanning healthy 
donated breast tissue from cancer-unaffected donors (HDB), contralateral unaffected breast 
(CUB), ipsilateral opposite quadrant (OQ), adjacent normal tissue (AN), and tumor (TU) 
samples. 

Overall, the dataset comprises \textbf{446 breast tissue samples} distributed across the 
five tissue categories listed below. 

\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item 182 HDB samples (healthy donated breast), representing cancer-unaffected breast tissue from donors and used as the physiological methylation baseline;
    \item 60 AN samples (adjacent-normal), corresponding to histologically normal tissue sampled near the tumor and capturing early epigenetic alterations along the tumor-proximity axis;
    \item 69 TU samples (tumor tissue), representing invasive breast cancer and defining the malignant endpoint of the axis;
    \item 67 OQ samples (ipsilateral opposite quadrant), benign tissue from the same breast but distant from the tumor, reflecting intermediate proximity-associated alterations;
    \item 68 CUB samples (contralateral unaffected breast), benign tissue from the opposite breast, representing a distal but still patient-matched non-malignant reference.
\end{itemize}
For all subsequent analyses, only the three biologically coherent classes (Normal, Adjacent, Tumor) are retained
while OQ and CUB samples are excluded, as they represent intermediate benign tissues along the tumor-proximity axis and do not align 
with the cross-dataset comparison framework adopted in this work.


All methylation levels are stored as $\beta$-values in $[0,1]$, 
yielding a compact working matrix of 311 tissue samples by 702{,}573 CpG probes.

% Missingness
% ============================================
\paragraph{Missingness filtering and imputation}
\label{par:gse287331_missingness}
I quantified the amount and structure of
missing values in the $\beta$-matrix. Probe-wise missingness is a well-known
source of unreliability in Illumina methylation arrays, and several studies recommend
removing CpGs that exhibit low measurement quality or an excessive proportion of missing
values. For example, Islam \textit{et al.}~\cite{islam2019_pediatric_dnamm} excluded probes
with missing $\beta$-values in more than $2\%$ of samples in their pediatric tissue
methylation analysis, while Mansell \textit{et al.}~\cite{mansell2019guidance} applied a
similar $1\%$ threshold for detection failure in their EPIC-array quality control
pipeline. 

Guided by this evidence and the large number of CpG sites available,
I adopted a stringent probe-wise filtering criterion and removed all
CpGs missing in more than $1\%$ of samples. This choice prioritises high-confidence
measurements while minimising reliance on imputed values.

After filtering, only a negligible fraction of values remained missing in the working
matrix (58\,561 NaN, corresponding to $0.0187\%$ of all entries). Given this extremely low
level of sparsity, median-based imputation is both statistically robust and
distribution-preserving. Following current recommendations, where missing methylation values
are replaced by probe-wise medians to avoid introducing group-specific bias, all residual
NaN were imputed using the median $\beta$-value of each CpG across samples. This approach is consistent with recent harmonization methods such as mLiftOver, 
where missing methylation values are explicitly replaced using probe-wise median 
substitution to preserve biologically plausible levels \cite{chen2024_mliftover}.

\paragraph{Evidence of Missing Infinium Type~I/II Probe Bias Correction}

During the exploratory analysis performed uniformly across all three datasets, the per-sample
mean $\beta$-value distributions (Fig.~\ref{fig:gse287331_mean_beta_1}, 
Fig.~\ref{fig:gse287331_mean_beta_2},

Whereas \texttt{GSE69914} and \texttt{GSE225845} displayed a single broad peak of global

This unusual right-shifted bimodality---largely absent in the other datasets---strongly indicated 
that a key preprocessing step, namely Infinium Type~I/II probe-design bias correction, had not

To confirm this hypothesis, I stratified all probes by Infinium design type (Type~I vs.~Type~II) 
using the official Illumina EPIC v1.0 manifest, obtained from the corresponding Bioconductor 
annotation package \cite{Illumina450kAnno}. Using this manifest, the $\beta$-value distributions 
were separated by probe design and inspected through a dedicated diagnostic plot 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse287331_type_bias_check}). According to the expected behaviour described by 
Teschendorff et~al.~\cite{Teschendorff2013BMIQ}, datasets that have undergone BMIQ
normalization should display nearly overlapping $\beta$-value distributions for the two probe types.

In contrast, the Q--Q plot comparing Type~I and Type~II probes revealed a markedly elevated 
median absolute deviation (median $|\Delta| = 0.380$), indicating a strong and systematic 
quantile-wise shift between the two designs. Such a deviation is fully incompatible with 
BMIQ-corrected data, which should instead exhibit deviations close to zero across all quantiles.  
After applying BMIQ, this deviation decreases only marginally ($|\Delta| : 0.380 \rightarrow 0.370$), 
confirming that the correction improves probe-type alignment but only to a limited extent.

This modest improvement is also clearly visible in the diagnostic density plots 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse287331_typeI_II_post}). After BMIQ, the rightmost portion of the distribution 
(near $\beta \approx 1$) shows Type~II probes becoming visibly more similar to Type~I, and the 
strong asymmetry between the two curves is partially reduced. Nevertheless, the overall shapes 
of the two probe distributions remain distinct, and the characteristic ``double-tail’’ pattern 
of the uncorrected dataset is still present after normalization. BMIQ therefore warps the 
distribution in the expected direction, but the magnitude of the adjustment is small and 
insufficient to reconcile the two probe types to the degree observed in datasets processed with 
a full bias-correction pipeline.

A similar behaviour can be observed in the per-sample mean $\beta$ distributions: after BMIQ, the 
two peaks become slightly higher and closer to one another 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gse287331_mean_beta_3_post}), indicating that the algorithm partially reduces the 
gap between the two dominant modes. However, even after correction, the distribution remains 
visibly and consistently \emph{bimodal}, suggesting that BMIQ mitigates only weakly the underlying 
discrepancy and that sample-level heterogeneity continues to dominate the global methylation 
structure.

Taken together, these observations provide clear visual evidence that no probe-design bias 
correction was applied in the original processing. To further support this conclusion, 
I examined the official publication~\cite{Lord2024GSE287331}. The paper carefully describes the acquisition, 
processing, and analytical use of Illumina EPIC $\beta$-values, but it does \emph{not} mention 
BMIQ, SWAN, noob, or any other method aimed at correcting Infinium probe-type bias.  
The absence of such a statement---combined with the strong visual mismatch between Type~I and 
Type~II probe distributions---confirms that no probe-design bias correction was applied before the 
release of the dataset.

For these reasons, applying a dedicated Infinium Type~I/II bias correction step is necessary 
before performing any comparative analysis.

\paragraph{BMIQ correction: implementation and methodological considerations}
\label{par:gse287331_bmiq}

Given the clear evidence of missing Infinium Type~I/II probe-bias correction, 
I applied the canonical BMIQ algorithm
\cite{Teschendorff2013BMIQ}, using the implementation provided by the 
\texttt{wateRmelon} package \cite{Pidsley2013wateRmelon}, to harmonize the 
distributions of Type~I and Type~II probes for each sample. To ensure full 
reproducibility and strict memory control on the extremely wide EPIC matrix, I implemented a \emph{sequential, sample-wise BMIQ correction}, 
avoiding any between-sample pooling or multi-core parallelization. Each sample’s 
$\beta$ vector was processed independently using a safe wrapper around 
\texttt{wateRmelon::BMIQ}, which first verified the availability of a sufficient 
number of non-missing Type~I and Type~II probes and then returned the corrected 
$\beta$-values. The corrected matrix was updated in place, preserving the schema 
\texttt{id\_tissue | CpGs... | label} and subsequently written to a single Parquet file. 
This design guarantees that no cross-sample information influenced the correction and 
that the warping applied by BMIQ reflects exclusively the distributional 
characteristics of each individual sample.

While BMIQ is widely used to mitigate probe-design bias, multiple studies have raised
important methodological caveats regarding normalization procedures that operate on
the full distribution of methylation values. Yousefi \textit{et al.}~\cite{Yousefi2013Normalization}
highlighted that quantile-based adjustments and related normalization strategies
may ``come at the cost of \emph{artificially reduced biological variability},'' 
a phenomenon that is particularly undesirable when studying biological groups expected 
to differ globally in their methylation profiles. Similarly, Liu \textit{et al.}
\cite{Liu2016Processing} reported that, when methylation alterations are extensive in size
and number, certain normalization methods can ``\emph{remove biological signal},'' 
effectively shrinking meaningful differences between groups. 

More recently, Welsh \textit{et al.}~\cite{Welsh2023EPICnorm} performed a systematic
evaluation of normalization methods for EPIC arrays and showed that approaches enforcing
strong distributional alignment may distort real biological structure. In particular,
the authors observed that quantile-based normalization ``\emph{forces the empirical 
marginal distributions of the samples to be the same, removing all variation in this
statistic},'' and reported that BMIQ, in some settings, yielded ``\emph{distorted and 
discontinuous}'' distributions. Such findings provide a conceptual explanation for the 
behaviour observed: although BMIQ was applied strictly 
per-sample, its within-array warping can attenuate global differences between 
Normal, Adjacent, and Tumor samples when these differences are manifested as broad 
shifts in the underlying $\beta$-value distribution.

Together, these considerations emphasise both the necessity and the limitations of
probe-design bias correction. Applying BMIQ was essential to remove the technical 
Type~I/II bias absent in the original processing, yet its known 
tendency to reduce distributional heterogeneity across groups must be carefully taken 
into account when interpreting group-level contrasts in downstream analyses.

Importantly, the modest degree of probe-type harmonization is accompanied by a noticeable 
flattening of biological structure: as will be shown in the low-dimensional embedding analysis 
(Section~\ref{par:gse287331_embeddings}), PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE projections reveal that the 
Normal, Adjacent, and Tumor groups become \emph{less clearly separable} after BMIQ.  
For this reason, all downstream analyses are carried out on the \textbf{non-BMIQ version} of the 
dataset, which preserves sharper cluster boundaries and better reflects the underlying biological 
signal.

group structure intrinsic to this dataset.

the average silhouette coefficient \emph{before} BMIQ was 
$\mathrm{sil}_{\text{pre}} = 0.2876$,  
but \emph{after} BMIQ correction it declined to 
$\mathrm{sil}_{\text{post}} = 0.2537$.  
Thus, BMIQ \emph{reduced}--even if not significantly--the natural clusterability of the data, collapsing genuine 
biological differences—particularly the clear separation between Normal and Adjacent 
samples.

For this reason, and in accordance with the principle of preserving authentic biological 
signal, the BMIQ-normalised version was not adopted.

% Summary

\paragraph{Overall interpretation}

The GSE287331 dataset provides a clean and well-structured EPIC methylation matrix for 
studying breast tissue along the tumour-proximity axis. After stringent $1\%$ probe-wise 
filtering and minimal median-based imputation ($0.0187\%$ NaN), the working matrix 
(311~samples $\times$ 702{,}573~CpGs) is essentially complete. Manifest annotation confirms 
the expected EPIC architecture dominated by Open Sea probes, followed by CpG islands, 
shores, and shelves.

Exploratory diagnostics revealed that the publicly released data lacked Infinium 
Type~I/II bias correction. BMIQ reduced probe-type discrepancies only slightly and 
decreased the silhouette score (from $0.2876$ to $0.2537$), indicating a loss of genuine 
biological separation. For this reason, all analyses in this thesis rely on the 
non-BMIQ version, which better preserves group structure.

Global methylation distributions display the typical bimodal EPIC profile with a subtle 
gradient (Normal $>$ Adjacent $>$ Tumor). Variance and correlation structures show strong 
similarity between Normal and Adjacent tissues and only mild variability increases in 
Tumor. In contrast, low-dimensional embeddings reveal clear separation of all three 
groups—especially between Normal and Adjacent—highlighting meaningful multivariate 
differences despite globally similar first- and second-order moments.

Locus-specific analyses show focused disruptions: Tumor exhibits widespread mild 
hypomethylation, whereas Adjacent tissue displays fewer but more pronounced deviations. 
Dynamic-network proxies further indicate a more compact perturbed core in Tumor samples 
and weaker, early-stage perturbations in Adjacent tissue, consistent with 
field-cancerization effects.

Overall, GSE287331 is a high-quality dataset with strong and biologically interpretable 
Normal–Adjacent–Tumor separation, making it well suited for downstream comparative and 
mechanistic analyses.

\section{Inter Dataset Analysis}
\label{sec:inter_dataset}

The objective of this section is not to merge the datasets---an approach that would be biologically and technically 
inappropriate due to differences in array platforms (HM450 vs.\ EPIC), preprocessing pipelines, and the strong 
intrinsic structure observed in GSE287331---but rather to compare their internal behaviours. 
This cross-dataset perspective assesses whether the early epigenetic alterations detected in the intra-dataset 
analyses are \emph{reproducible across independent cohorts}, thereby supporting the central biological premise 
of this thesis: detecting early methylation drift in histologically normal tissues and evaluating its potential 
role in tumour initiation.

\subsection{Rationale}

Across all three datasets, the intra-dataset analyses revealed three highly consistent phenomena:

\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item Normal and Adjacent tissues are globally similar, with nearly superimposed $\beta$-value distributions and
    comparable CpG-wise variance profiles.
    \item Tumour samples show increased heterogeneity and a clear bias toward hypomethylation, although the magnitude
    of this shift varies across datasets.
    \item Adjacent tissues exhibit early methylation drift, detectable through $\Delta\beta$ distributions and outlier 
    profiles even when global metrics remain highly similar to Normal.
\end{itemize}

These patterns align with the expected biological progression from Normal to Tumour, in which epigenetic 
deregulation arises gradually and affects normal-appearing tissue surrounding the tumour.  
The inter-dataset analysis therefore evaluates the \textit{directional consistency} of these effects, rather than aiming 
for quantitative comparability.

Table~\ref{tab:inter_summary_NA} reports a set of quantitative metrics designed to characterise 
the epigenetic deviation of Adjacent tissue from the Normal methylome across the three cohorts.  
Across all datasets, these metrics collectively highlight the presence of early epigenetic drift 
in histologically non-tumour tissue, consistent with the field-defect framework described by 
Teschendorff et~al.~\cite{teschendorff2016} and recently reinforced by studies in other 
tissue contexts.



\paragraph{Mean absolute methylation difference, $\text{Mean}|\Delta\beta|(\text{A--N})$}  
For each CpG I computed the absolute mean difference between Adjacent and Normal:
\[
|\Delta\beta_i| = \left| \beta^{(A)}_i - \beta^{(N)}_i \right|.
\]
The cross-cohort pattern shows progressively larger locus-specific shifts from GSE69914 
(0.0090) to GSE225845 (0.0148) and GSE287331 (0.0306).  
This monotonic increase reflects a strengthening early deviation in Adjacent tissues.  
Similar locus-wise methylation drifts in histologically normal tissue have been reported in 
independent systems, such as colonic mucosa adjacent to adenomas~\cite{Yates2024_NormalMucosaField}, 
supporting the interpretation of these alterations as early signs of field cancerization.

\paragraph{Global variance ratio, $\mathrm{Var(A)/Var(N)}$}  
For each sample $s$, the global methylation variance is computed across all CpG sites:
\[
v_s = \mathrm{Var}\!\left(\beta_{s,1}, \beta_{s,2}, \ldots, \beta_{s,m}\right),
\]
where $m$ denotes the number of CpGs.  
Group-wise mean variances are then obtained as
\[
\overline{v}^{(N)} = \frac{1}{|N|} \sum_{s \in N} v_s,
\qquad
\overline{v}^{(A)} = \frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{s \in A} v_s,
\]
and the reported metric is their ratio:
\[
\mathrm{Var(A)/Var(N)} = 
\frac{\overline{v}^{(A)}}{\overline{v}^{(N)}}.
\]

Across all datasets this ratio remained consistently close to~1 (0.93--0.99), indicating that 
Adjacent tissue does not exhibit diffuse genome-wide instability.  
This aligns with recent evidence that early pre-neoplastic alterations tend to be \emph{focal} 
rather than global, with only specific CpG sites showing abnormal dispersion while overall 
variance remains preserved~\cite{Yates2024_NormalMucosaField}.  
Such behaviour is consistent with contemporary models of early tumourigenesis, in which subtle, 
locally restricted perturbations accumulate prior to large-scale chromatin deregulation 
and overt neoplastic transformation~\cite{Zhang2024_TumorInitiationEpigenetic}.



\paragraph{Outlier burden ratio, A/N}  
For each sample, the outlier burden is defined as the number of CpG sites whose methylation 
deviates from the Normal reference distribution by more than three times the Normal 
interquartile range:
\[
\text{outlier}_s = 
\#\left\{\, i \;:\; 
\left| \beta_{i,s} - \tilde{\beta}^{(N)}_i \right| 
> 3 \cdot IQR^{(N)}_i \,\right\},
\]
where $\tilde{\beta}^{(N)}_i$ and $IQR^{(N)}_i$ denote the median and interquartile range of CpG $i$ 
across Normal samples.  
The reported A/N ratio corresponds to the mean outlier burden in Adjacent tissue divided by the 
mean burden in Normal tissue.  
This metric shows the strongest cross-dataset gradient: from mild enrichment in GSE69914 
(A/N $\approx 1.35$), to moderate imbalance in GSE225845 (A/N $\approx 2.79$), to a striking 
amplification in GSE287331 (A/N $\approx 13.33$).  
These findings closely mirror evidence that increased local methylation variability in 
histologically normal tissue is a sensitive marker of early instability~\cite{Yates2024_NormalMucosaField}, 
and provide quantitative support for the presence of pronounced field effects in GSE287331.





\paragraph{Silhouette score (Normal vs.\ Adjacent)}  
For each sample $s$, let $a(s)$ denote the mean distance from $s$ to all other samples in the 
same group (cohesion), and let $b(s)$ denote the smallest mean distance from $s$ to samples in 
any different group (separation).  
The silhouette coefficient for sample $s$ is defined as
\[
\mathrm{sil}(s) =
\frac{b(s) - a(s)}{\max\{a(s),\, b(s)\}},
\]
and the reported score corresponds to the mean silhouette across all Normal and Adjacent 
samples.  
Higher values indicate clearer multivariate separation, whereas values near zero reflect 
substantial overlap.

GSE69914 (0.0028) and GSE225845 (0.0423) show almost complete overlap between Normal and 
Adjacent, consistent with minimal global displacement in PCA space.  
In contrast, GSE287331 exhibits partial separation (0.3442), indicating that the epigenetic 
landscape of Adjacent tissue is substantially shifted relative to Normal.  
This behaviour aligns with mechanistic models in which epigenetic dysregulation constitutes 
one of the earliest events in tumour initiation~\cite{Zhang2024_TumorInitiationEpigenetic}, 
preceding morphological transformation.

Together, these metrics depict a consistent Normal--Adjacent gradient across datasets.  
Early epigenetic drift is detectable in all cohorts, but its magnitude, focality, and 
multivariate visibility intensify markedly from GSE69914 to GSE287331.  
Importantly, the combination of stable global variance with strong outlier enrichment 
supports a model in which early pre-neoplastic changes arise through \emph{localised} 
perturbations—a hallmark of field cancerization~\cite{Yates2024_NormalMucosaField, teschendorff2016}.

\subsection{Interpretation and implications}

Taken together, the cross-dataset metrics reveal that the epigenetic deviations observed in 
Adjacent tissue are not idiosyncratic artefacts of a single cohort, but represent 
\emph{coherent and reproducible biological signals}.  
Although the intensity of the effect varies across datasets, the directionality is remarkably 
consistent:

\begin{itemize}[label=-]
    \item Normal and Adjacent tissues remain globally similar at the whole-methylome level;
    \item Adjacent samples consistently exhibit early drift, detectable through locus-specific
          shifts, focal outlier enrichment, and mild multivariate displacement;
    \item Tumour samples show the expected pattern of strong hypomethylation and increased 
          heterogeneity.
\end{itemize}

A key implication of this comparison is that the \emph{strength} of the Normal--Adjacent signal 
differs substantially across cohorts.  
In GSE69914 and GSE225845 the drift is detectable but modest, whereas GSE287331 displays a 
pronounced field-defect signature across all metrics: larger locus-specific shifts, extreme 
outlier enrichment, and measurable separation in multivariate space.  
This makes GSE287331 the most informative dataset for downstream algorithmic exploration, as it 
provides the strongest and clearest Normal--Adjacent contrast from which to extract early 
epigenetic markers.

For this reason, GSE287331 is treated as the \emph{primary discovery dataset} for subsequent 
analyses, while GSE69914 and GSE225845 are used as \emph{external validation replication cohorts}.  
Importantly, in this work the term “validation” refers to \emph{biological} replication across 
independent datasets—rather than machine-learning validation.  
The discovery step is carried out in GSE287331, and the resulting CpG markers or patterns are 
then evaluated in the two additional cohorts to test whether the same loci display consistent 
Normal--Adjacent drift across different platforms, sample compositions, and preprocessing 
pipelines. 

Because the three cohorts differ in array technology, processing pipelines, and study design, 
merging them would not be biologically meaningful; instead, the inter-dataset analysis serves as 
a multi-cohort consistency check, supporting the interpretation that the Normal$\rightarrow$Adjacent 
drift represents genuine early epigenetic deregulation rather than dataset-specific noise.  
This framework aligns with contemporary models of tumour initiation, in which subtle, locally 
restricted perturbations arise in histologically normal tissue prior to overt neoplastic 
transformation.
